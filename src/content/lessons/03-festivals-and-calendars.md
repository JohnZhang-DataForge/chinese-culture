## Two ways of marking time

Many Chinese festivals follow dates in a traditional **lunisolar calendar**, which relates lunar months to the solar year. Because it is not the same as the Gregorian calendar used internationally, a festival can fall on a different Gregorian date from one year to the next.

A festival guide tells you about its cultural background; an **event announcement** tells you about a particular gathering at a particular time. The two are not interchangeable.

## Three festivals to recognize

- **Spring Festival (春节 / 春節, Chūnjié):** the start of the traditional new year, often associated with reunion, greetings and household preparations.
- **Dragon Boat Festival (端午节 / 端午節, Duānwǔ Jié):** commonly observed on the fifth day of the fifth lunar month, with racing and regional foods such as *zòngzi*.
- **Mid-Autumn Festival (中秋节 / 中秋節, Zhōngqiū Jié):** associated with moon-viewing and family connections, on the fifteenth day of the eighth lunar month.

Celebrations change from region to region and across generations. Not every family observes every practice.

## A helpful distinction: legend and history

Stories about the poet Qu Yuan or the moon figure Chang'e are important parts of festival culture. They are **traditional narratives**, not necessarily single, proven historical explanations for every practice.
