## Writing you can see

In Chinese calligraphy, a character is meaningful language and a carefully shaped visual composition. The direction and pressure of a brush can make a stroke seem energetic, delicate, balanced or spontaneous.

Writers have developed several major script traditions, among them seal, clerical, standard, running and cursive scripts. These are not simply fonts; they reflect changing practices, visual preferences and ways of writing.

## Four things to notice

1. **Stroke:** A line can begin heavily, move lightly and end decisively.
2. **Rhythm:** Repeated marks can feel measured or quick.
3. **Space:** Empty paper is part of the arrangement.
4. **Context:** An inscription on a handscroll, a public sign and an intimate letter invite different kinds of reading.

Calligraphy has been practiced in many social settings and continues in contemporary art and education.

## Looking at a museum example

The Metropolitan Museum of Art's essay on calligraphy introduces scripts, material tools and historical objects. Pay attention to how writing also connects to poetry, painting and the circulation of letters.

## Questions to try

Can you tell which marks appear more deliberate or fluid? What can you learn from the use of empty space even before you can read the words?
