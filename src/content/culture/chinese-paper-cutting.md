## A picture made by removing paper

Chinese paper-cutting (剪纸, jiǎnzhǐ) turns a sheet of paper into an image through cutting, piercing and shaping. The design is formed not only by the paper that remains, but also by the openings and spaces created around it. Patterns may be simple or highly detailed, and the finished work can be displayed on windows, walls, doors and other everyday surfaces.

UNESCO inscribed Chinese paper-cut on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2009. The practice is found across China and among different ethnic groups; techniques and motifs vary by place and purpose.

## Tools, techniques and patterns

Some makers fold paper before cutting so that a repeated design appears when it is opened. Others work on a flat sheet, cut directly with scissors or use a knife or chisel-like tool for finer details. Paper may remain its original colour or be dyed and layered.

Motifs can include flowers, animals, plants, people, symbols and scenes from daily life. A design made for a wedding may communicate wishes for happiness; a seasonal decoration may welcome a festival or mark a change in the year. These meanings are not fixed in every region or household.

## A living craft

Knowledge has often been passed through families and local communities, including from older makers to younger learners. Today, artists also experiment with new materials, digital design and contemporary subjects. New tools do not erase the tradition; they show how a familiar craft can keep changing.

## Look closely

Hold a paper-cut against the light. Notice how the solid shapes and open spaces depend on one another. Which parts connect the image, and which details would disappear if one small bridge of paper were cut away?
