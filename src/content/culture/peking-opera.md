## A stage built from signs

Peking opera (京剧, Jīngjù) combines singing, recitation, acting and martial movement. It became widely known in the nineteenth century. UNESCO inscribed it on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2010. Stories may draw on history, literature, politics, society and everyday life.

## Four kinds of stage skill

A traditional description emphasizes *chang, nian, zuo* and *da*: singing, spoken or chanted delivery, acting and movement or martial skills. These are not separate boxes in every scene. A performer may move from speech into song, then use a stylized gesture to suggest an action the audience imagines. A turn of the body, hand position or glance can communicate intention and emotion.

## Role types and visual language

Traditional role categories include *sheng* (male roles), *dan* (female roles), *jing* (painted-face roles) and *chou* (comic roles). Each contains subtypes. These are theatrical conventions, not simple descriptions of performers themselves. Costumes and facial make-up help audiences recognize a character's dramatic role, but their meanings depend on context; a single colour is not a universal code.

## Music and the audience

The *jinghu*, a high-pitched bowed string instrument, is closely associated with Peking opera. Flutes and other strings may accompany lyrical scenes, while drums, gongs and cymbals energize action. You do not need to understand every word to begin watching: notice how music signals a change and how a gesture carries meaning.
