## From earth to a finished vessel

Porcelain is a type of ceramic valued for its strength, fine texture and, in some forms, light-transmitting quality. Its appearance depends on the materials, the shape of the object, the glaze, the decoration and the firing process. It is not simply “white pottery”: porcelain can be plain, painted, coloured or highly elaborate.

The Smithsonian's National Museum of Asian Art describes porcelain production through four broad stages: forming, glazing, decorating and firing. The exact materials and sequence vary by workshop, product and period.

## The work of making porcelain

A maker first prepares and shapes the ceramic body. The piece is dried and may be trimmed or refined before glaze is applied. Decoration can be painted onto the surface or added through other techniques. Firing in a kiln transforms the prepared object into a hard ceramic vessel. The temperature and atmosphere of the kiln affect the final colour, texture and strength.

Kaolin is an important ingredient in many porcelain bodies, but recipes and materials are not identical across all periods and production centers. The craft depends on practical knowledge of clay, glaze, tools and kiln conditions.

## Jingdezhen and a wider world

Jingdezhen, in Jiangxi province, became a major center of China's ceramics industry. The Smithsonian notes its importance in global ceramic production from the fourteenth century and the expansion of trade in the sixteenth century. Porcelain made in China travelled through trade networks and influenced ceramic makers elsewhere.

Jingdezhen's history is not the whole story of Chinese ceramics. Many regions developed their own kilns, materials, shapes and decorative traditions.

## Look beyond the surface

When you see a porcelain bowl or vase, notice its weight, translucency, glaze and painted details. Ask how the shape was formed and what the firing process might have changed. A finished object holds traces of material choices and skilled work that are easy to overlook.
